PORTFOLIO RESUME ABOUT ERIC D'SA
UC BERKELEY SOCIAL SPACES LIFESTYLYE CHANGES FOR STUDENTS  
This project originates from UGBA 190T (Needfinding), a class offered at UC Berkeley that is a replica of the class taught by Michael Barry at Stanford's d.school. Our capstone project was a user research project, investigating how students cope, stay sane, or thrive at UC Berkeley. Collaborators: Chris Poulsen, Jocelyn Nguyen, Marcus van Duren
Tools: Adobe Illustrator, Interviewing, 2x2 Frameworks INTERVIEW GUIDELINES + PROCESS We had multiple rounds of interviews, and thus made some guidelines for conducting our interviews. We ended up with the following:

Time: 1 hour, in person interviews
Interviewers: 3 people (1 to interview, 1 to take notes, 1 to handle video recording)

We recorded videos of our interviews (with permission). After the interview, we would listen to the interview again, and write down our our notes and realizations that we would use later in our analysis.

Our goal was to keep the interview as conversational as possible, and dive very deep into insights once we knew the interviewee well enough. Our general format for our interview was the following:

30 min (open, unstructured conversation): get to know the interviewee better, build trust to ask harder questions later, learning more about the interviewee to ask questions tailored to their experiences
15 min (questions for insights): asking the main questions we had in mind coming to the interview, and gaining initial insights
15 min (follow up to answers): based on the interviewee's answers, we asked different questions or asked to go more in depth in the stories the interviewees provided

Although we had this format going into the interview, we did not strictly follow these time limits. If we needed less time for getting to know someone but more time for followup, we adjusted time on the fly. Our highest priority was hearing as many stories and experiences about our interviewee, and we reorganized our time as needed to do so.

 INTERVIEWING: ROUND 1 For our first round of interviews, we kept our questions very general and open-ended. Although we had our own guesses based on our experiences as UC Berkeley students, we didn't want to assume that was the experience of every person.

Thus, our questions were mostly introductive, asking for people's lifestyles as students. Key things included their sleeping and eating patterns, where they studied, places they went when they were stressed, and when they were around people vs being alone.

Our main conclusions from this round of interviews was that most people recognized their stress points highest when they were studying at the library. This also stemmed from them reaching out to other people to study with them, to alleviate their stress. In other words, having people around them while they studied made their work fun instead of stressful. For our next round of interviews, we decided to focus on friends and support groups as ways of handling stress. INTERVIEWING: ROUND 2 Our second round of interviews focused on friendships that people made during their college experience, and how those friendships were formed. Interviewees were mostly grouped into two main categories:

1) Random Occurences: People who met their closest friends through seemingly random occurences, from sharing cookies on a dorm floor to mutual friends while house hunting.
2) Consistent Occurences: People who met their friends through consistent, frequently occuring activities. In this group, people bonded over a long period of time, reinforced by regular meetings.

Creating a solution to help people meet through random occurences was a formidable task, and we weren't confident it would be effective. We chose to focus on people who formed their friendships through meeting particular people on a regular basis. INTERVIEWING: ROUND 3 Our final round of interviews was purely focused on friendships formed by consistent meetings with other people. Our results were that consistent, easily accessible activities are the most effective for helping people bond. People get recommendations from friends on activities to do, and as they spend time together doing activities, people form close connections. INTERVIEW ANALYSIS We rewatched all our interviews, and added to our insights + notes for each interview. Then, we grouped our insights into categories, and assessed each category. Friendships stood out as a common theme for staying sane and thriving in the university. Many reasons interviewees gave centered around having a support group as well as having a clear identity that grew throughout their time at school. 2x2 FRAMEWORK We played with a couple of different axes for our 2x2 (when people meet each other, what kinds of situations bring people together, etc.). We found consistent meetings with others vs one off meetings to be a recurring pattern in our interviews. A lot of people either found each other with a random occurance in a dorm or dining hall, then didn't consciously reinforce that meeting in a friendship but randomly bumped into each other on campus. Another set of people found friends through consistent habits that brought people together (classes, clubs, working at the same places). This defined our first axis: anomaly vs norm (random vs consistent meetings).

Two types of friendships also recurred in our interviews. The first was a type of friendship that reinforced something already existent in you (e.g. favorite hobbies, classes for your major). These friendships were made between two people who were very similar in their tastes and interests. The other type of friendship was with someone that was very different from you, someone that added to your personality that did not exist before. This defined our second axis: identity-affirming vs. identity-shaping.

These two axes formed our four quadrants of analysis. Quadrants 1 and 4 involved anomalies, meetings and one-off occurences. While these situations occur almost all the time, they are hard to make a solution for.

Quadrant 3, friendships formed on identity affirming, recurring meetings, are friendships that the university supports well already. From classes to intramural sports, there are many activities on campus that bring together people with common interests. Normally, a student wants to do an activity, finds a place on campus that supports it, and joins, meeting other people who also went through the same process.

Quadrant 4, friendships formed on identity shaping, recurring meetings, was not something we heard about commonly in our interviews. We chose to address this group of friendships in our future observations and recommendations. DISPLACEMENT + FINAL ANALYSIS Current spaces on campus are open, flexible spaces with no intended purpose. People congregate and spend time together, but they need a reason to come together; they won't easily sit down somewhere and spend time together (university culture of always doing something / being productive). This ends in the majority of people studying together (being productive + being with other people). The vast majority of campus spaces are like this, leading to disproportionately more academic work, and less of other activities. For these other activities, they were mostly formed with people that shared common interests (like intramural sports).

Ideally, we would want someone to be able to walk into a random activity, meet strangers, and do this every week, in the act of meeting new people through very new activities. This would form more identity shaping friendships made through consistent meetings. FINAL RECOMMENDATION UC Berkeley needs a space on campus for students to congregate and meet each other. It needs to be designated purely as a non-academic space.

It also should intentionally avoid the open, flexible space that is prevalent in the rest of the campus. The current student union follows this standard of open, flexible space. It first two floors consist purely of multiple tables and chairs spread through a large space. This has led to it being used as an area with extra tables to study on rather than an area for people to relax and enjoy themselves.

To designate this space (or new student union) as purely for social purposes, it would need many different activities in its space. While not an exhaustive list, other schools have used the following for similar efforts:

1) Campus pub
2) Rock climbing
3) Arcade lounge
4) Food court
5) Bowling
6) Billiards

All these activities share the act of bringing relative strangers together through one experience that they can come back to and repeat together. UC Berkeley needs a space on campus that hosts these types of activities.
